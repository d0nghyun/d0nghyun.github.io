"So, what do you do?" That was the hardest question I knew. Seven years
of doing a bit of everything, and nothing I could be called.

It scared me enough to leave. Then the seat I landed in turned out to
want exactly that kind of person.

## The label

A portfolio manager talks in book size and returns. An engineer talks in
the size and depth of the problems they solved. I had neither language.

Not for lack of depth, I think. I did what the company needed. Data when
it needed data, alpha when it needed alpha, SI when it needed SI, then a
new AI business. The seat was part strategy office, part research lab.

The culture suited me. Nobody fenced me in, and what I wanted to do and
what was needed kept lining up. That doesn't mean it was easy — everyone
was struggling in their own seat. Still, seven years in a place like
that was luck.

But work that way and you don't end up with a name for yourself. A small
market makes it worse. Seats that mix finance, quant, and AI were rare
in Korea. The times are heading this way, so it'll widen soon — that's
what I kept choosing to believe, for seven years.

## The irony

At the new company I was put on a team that's starting something new.

New work has to be built up piece by piece, together. Nothing is settled
yet, so you end up touching a bit of everything — which is how I worked
for seven years. The track record I feared made me neither-this-nor-that
looks, here, like a qualification.

The name can come later. Build the thing and it follows; if it doesn't,
I'll think about that then.

It also settles what to build here. Before, I built things where the
degrees of freedom were infinite. This time it's inside a structure, and
the thing has to run on that structure. That's what I came to try, and
it's not a one-person job.

## What to attempt

I'm writing down now what to look at in six months. Decide the criteria
later and I'd fit them to whatever happened — that's not a
retrospective, that's self-justification.

If I'm answering "what did you do here" with a list again, I've repeated
the seven years. It has to be an outcome I can point to, not a run-down
of tasks.

That isn't a one-person job either. Build it with the team, and it has
to actually run.

Six months may be too early to tell. Then I'll write that it was too
early to tell.

That's it for looking back. Time to get absorbed in the new company and
the new project.
