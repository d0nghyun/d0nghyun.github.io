I sat down to write what seven years taught me and got a list of tools.
Pipelines, backtests, a platform, a team. That's a résumé, not a
retrospective.

What was left was what I learned about myself. Mostly of the *I thought
so, turns out not* variety. The biggest change was what I count as an
asset. This goes in the order I got there.

## How the range widened

Do analysis and sooner or later the data quality starts bothering you. Go fix
the quality and the slow code in the pipeline starts bothering you. Fix
the pipeline and it turns out the backtest has been returning wrong
numbers. Dig into one thing and the floor under it opens up.

At a big company this is where ownership splits and you go ask another
team. At a small one you do it yourself. Mine was the second kind. Nobody
fenced off scope; they let me go where I wanted.

So it went like this. CPU-bound, memory-bound — I never studied any of
it and I don't know the theory. I do measure, though. Once I know which
one it is, I go look it up case by case. There was no GPT back then.
What I had were colleagues who answered when asked, and answered well.
A bit like having an AI on hand.

It still works that way. Last month I was building a funding model and
found that the backtest had never once subtracted funding cost. There
was a setting to turn it on and off, and no code that read the setting.
Wiring it properly and re-running reordered the strategies and flipped
one signal into a loss. I went in to build a model and came out having
fixed the cost code.

Seven years of that and the ground I'd covered had gotten wide. I didn't
widen it on purpose. I don't know which way is the good way. I just
think I kept making the best call available at each spot.

So why did I go that far.

## Focus as an excuse

Woke up and worked, went in and worked, came home and worked. I called it
focus, and it was also an excuse. I thought it was a place where you had
no choice.

Same after I left. New job, and I'd still be holding onto something after
hours. Nobody asking, no extra pay. Ah — the company wasn't what made me
do it. I just find it fun. The part where everything else goes quiet and
there's one thing.

I start before everything is ready. Once I'm executing it's the opposite
— I cut the problem down very narrow and dig only there. When it gives, I go start something else. That
narrow stretch is the fun part.

What I'm building now went the same way. Not finished and then switched
on; switched on and then fixed. Things I should have caught before
turning it on.

## What counts as an asset

When I decided to leave, I wrote that my career and my finances were all
in the same asset class. Back then I counted assets as the bank balance
and the career.

It's a bit different now. Whatever keeps me able to do what I want, when I
want — that's the asset, I think. Money, reputation, a track record,
anything. I'd rather not worry about money, though.

I want to enjoy it. And leave something good behind if I can. The skills I
use I mostly pick up by building something with them, so I keep picking
things up and writing down what I learn. This blog is one of those.

The main thing is the new job, of course. I came to learn what I hadn't
done before, so that is what I have to build here. What I pick up outside
is for using here anyway.

I've never actually written it down, though. Kept in my head it blurs,
and blurred means it doesn't get done. That's the next post.

For now there's nothing to do but do it well.
