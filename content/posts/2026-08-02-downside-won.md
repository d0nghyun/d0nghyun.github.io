On July 1st, 2026, I changed jobs for the first time. The company I
joined in January 2019 changed its name in a 2021 merger, and I stayed
in the same seat for seven years. My last title was team lead, and the
product I had led from day one was in full swing, getting good
reviews.

## The payoff of leaving

Work as a quant long enough and you end up drawing a payoff diagram
for everything. A job change was no exception.

Staying was a leveraged long. If the product succeeded it would be my
story, and the upside was wide open — but the downside was fully
exposed too. A startup's volatility doesn't belong to the company
alone; once you're the team lead, it is simply your volatility.

Leaving was the opposite. A solid floor, an above-average mean, but no
open ceiling. Taking stability in exchange for letting go of the big
win.

The same payoff is worth different amounts to different holders,
though. So the question was: which side should the current me be
holding?

## A different profile

If I were a few years younger. If I were still single. If I had even
one big-company stint behind me.

Any one of those and I would have stayed. None were true. I had
recently married, and working through the night every day carried a
weight it never used to. My career and my finances were all in the
same asset class — a portfolio with no diversification whatsoever
(what would I have said about someone else's portfolio like that?). I
also knew in my body what a few burned years cost: the rebound always
arrives, and I lacked the confidence to pay that invoice one more
time. In early 2026 alone, I was unwell three or four times.

The payoff hadn't changed. My profile had.

Strangely, the identity question weighed the most. In seven years I
did everything — data pipelines, analytics, quant research, an AI
platform, team leading. Having done everything also means being hard
to call any one thing. Not a manager running serious money, not an
engineer handling serious traffic; AI person, data person? If the
product succeeds, the ambiguity becomes a story. If it just fades out?
What did I do all those years?

Ah, so this is what scares me. That's when I knew.

One more thing: the deeper we go into the AI era, the more curious I
became about structure. Startups are high-entropy places to begin
with, and AI worked in the direction of raising that entropy further.
After seven years of unlimited degrees of freedom, I wanted to watch
how a structured organization actually runs, from the inside.

## The ticket I returned

If I name the one thing I gave up, it's clear: the chance to work with
well-known hedge funds, and a possible path to the US. A ticket I
could never create as an individual — you have to be in that seat to
hold it — and I handed it back. Do I regret it? Yes. A lot.

Isn't it irresponsible for the first leader of a product in full swing
to walk out now? That was the question I held until the end. Nobody
blamed me; I just asked myself, daily.

A payoff diagram doesn't hand you the answer. It only tells you
exactly what you sold and what you bought. I sold the upside and
bought the floor. Without that frame, I would still be weighing the
two.

Sometimes what's needed is to throw yourself into an irrational
adventure no one else understands. I couldn't make that throw one more
time — but I leave with more than I came with.

What seven years taught me goes in the next post.
