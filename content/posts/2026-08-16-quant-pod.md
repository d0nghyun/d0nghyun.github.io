The diagram below is the map of the AI quant pod I'm building. An LLM wrote most
of the code. What matters isn't the stack itself but the philosophy the diagram
carries — if you run systematic trading with LLM agents, where do I actually have
to look, and what can I hand over? P&L, costs and drawdowns are covered separately
in the [monthly operations](/posts/2026-08-03-monthly-operations/) and

## One pod, one strategy concept first

Everything inside the frame exists to develop a single concept of strategy:
crypto perpetual futures (futures you hold indefinitely, no expiry), traded
cross-sectionally long-short — compare names against each other, buy the
relatively cheap, sell the relatively expensive — on hourly bars, with no bet on
market direction (market-neutral). That one line is the pod's strategy concept
(the figure calls it the mandate). Without it, the same drawing could be a stock
picker or a daily-bar trend book, and everything downstream — bar cadence,
survivorship handling, risk brakes — would have come out different, or diverged.

Nailing the concept down is also what separates the reusable parts from the rest
at the next pod launch — the dashed outlines stacked behind the frame, the pods
that don't exist yet. Korean equities will probably be the second pod, and it
won't be this pod stretched: a different concept, the know-how carried over, the
same drawing done again faster. Whether that's another Mac mini or a cloud box.

## Three members

The work is split between the computer, the agents, and me, by the nature of
the work.

| Member | Nature | Owns | Fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Computer | deterministic · 24/7 · zero creativity | the deterministic pipeline — collection, strategy execution, validation, orders | loudly, reproducibly |
| Agents | creative · tireless · often wrong | research — ideas, tests, records, proposals | plausibly |
| Me | slow · judging · owns the risk | the hard-to-reverse calls — tolerance, kill rulings, live allocation | late, and big |

There is nothing creative in the computer's share, which is exactly why it can be
trusted with the path the money takes. The agents write every verdict into a
research journal — the mediocre ones kept too, never deleted — but promoting
anything to live is off limits. A proposal goes to a validation gate, and the
gate re-runs the candidate's backtest from scratch, deterministically. Recent
data doesn't get a vote in the registration numbers — everything past a cutoff
date is frozen as a holdout and used only as the real report card after
registration. And once proposals pile up into the several hundreds, something
that looks good by pure chance is guaranteed to appear, so individual strategy
numbers get discounted accordingly and the judgment happens at the book level.
The checks are computing code, not prompts or LLM hooks. And live decisions are
made by my hand only.

The point is that each member fails differently, and they cover each other's
blind spots. What is wired to what, and what is conspicuously not wired — that
pipeline is the answer to "how far do you let it in."

## What I delegated to the LLM, and what I didn't

"An LLM wrote most of the code" hides the actual experience. My verification
effort was severely uneven, on purpose. What matters isn't the dashboard code —
it's the backtest logic the dashboard is drawing, and what the monitoring is
supposed to watch.

| Component | Written by | How hard I verified |
|---|---|---|
| Backtest + validation gate | LLM, to my spec | **Relentlessly.** This is where I stared. Deploys are deterministic re-runs, results are cross-checked against the registered cards, and fills are checked continuously against expected costs (TCA). |
| Data collection | LLM | Intensively at first — integrity checked by hand until the shapes held — then I had it build monitoring validation and stopped looking. |
| Allocation | — | Equal weight. Correlation against existing strategies is filtered at registration, so allocation itself carries no judgment. |
| Risk overlay | Me | Deliberately simple, set by hand to my own tolerance. Simple enough to need no verifying. |
| Order manager | LLM | Soaked on testnet with me watching the outputs, then promoted. Every fill is still checked against what the model expected to pay. |
| Monitoring | LLM | Barely. I said roughly what I wanted and took what came back. |

**I verified in proportion to how fast a mistake turns into a loss, and how
quietly it dies.** One story from each side of that line.

**Where the loose side nearly bit.** A process health check one day printed
`pid=None` for every process — including the order manager that sends real
orders — which is exactly what a silently dead process looks like. It turned out
to be no incident at all: the check was reading the wrong field name out of the
pm2 response. The tell was the uniformity — when every row of a probe prints the
same ominous value, the problem is usually the probe, not the system. Monitoring
built loosely produces alarms you have to diagnose before you can trust.

**Where the stared-at side paid off.** An agent once ran a backtest across
thirty parameter variants. It ran the engine correctly — then recomputed the
summary statistics by hand off the equity curve, and reported a max-drawdown
improvement double what the engine says. It got caught for one reason: the
performance record frozen at registration didn't match the research table. Every
registered strategy freezes its evidence at deploy time, and that evidence can
be regenerated deterministically at any moment. When two numbers that should be
identical aren't, something is wrong somewhere — and the frozen side tells you
which number to trust.

## Why a month was enough

All told it took about a month of weekday evenings and weekends. The same system
as a proper K8s deployment — manifests, images, a registry, an observability
stack — would have run past three months, and at one-person scale I have no
budget and only need low downtime anyway. pm2 restarts the processes, parquet
files on an SSD are a perfectly good object store, and static HTML is a
perfectly good dashboard.

Most of that month went where the table says I stared — the harness. Tests that
fail loudly, hooks that block the known mistakes, a validation gate that re-runs
everything. That's what makes delegating the rest survivable: I don't have to
trust the LLM's code, because every part that could lose money quietly has a
check I stared at attached to it.

## Limits, and what's next

This structure is a snapshot, not a finish line. There are roughly four axes to
push along.

**The pod axis.** There is the XTX way — pour in GPUs and data and cover fifty
thousand instruments with a single research stack — but I have to work with
limited data and limited compute. So the shape becomes one-concept pods, added
one at a time per universe and asset class. Korean equities are probably next.

**The computing axis.** Validation and backtesting keep improving. Every
methodology added makes the gate tighter, and the survival rate of the same
agent's proposals becomes information in itself.

**The agentic workflow axis.** I'm also experimenting with letting the agents
drift across topics on their own, toward wherever looks more promising.

**The data axis.** In my experience so far, registration success jumped when a
new kind of data opened up — not when the ideas got cleverer. Adding data is the
most reliable breakthrough there is.

All four axes point at the same spot. Keep pouring tokens in and mediocre
strategies keep coming out, until at some point more tokens stop producing
anything new — the marginal utility of research. Everyone's average is rising;
the edge comes from breaking through it. What I expect from the agents and what
I don't, and what actually breaks that marginal utility — that's the next post.
